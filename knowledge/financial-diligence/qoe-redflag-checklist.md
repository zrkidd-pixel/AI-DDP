# Quality of Earnings (QoE) Red-Flag Checklist

Referenced by: Financial Diligence / QoE Agent, Underwriting Agent, Verification Agent

A vendor-reported or company-reported EBITDA figure is a starting point, not a
number to underwrite on. This is the workstream a classroom screening exercise
can skip (public financials are taken at face value) but real diligence cannot —
a bad EBITDA number poisons every downstream calculation (EV, leverage, equity
check) without the model itself ever showing an error.

## The core question this workstream answers

Not "what is EBITDA," but **"what would a buyer's normalized, run-rate EBITDA be
if this business changed hands tomorrow, stripped of one-time, non-recurring, and
owner-specific items?"** The gap between reported and adjusted EBITDA is often
the single biggest swing factor in a deal's actual purchase multiple.

## Standard EBITDA adjustment categories (and the defensibility test for each)

- **Owner/related-party compensation normalization.** Common in founder-led or
  family-owned targets: above- or below-market owner salary, family members on
  payroll in no-show or overpaid roles, related-party leases or contracts at
  off-market rates. *Defensibility test:* is there a documented market-rate
  comparable, and would a professional replacement manager cost more or less?
- **One-time / non-recurring items.** Litigation settlements, restructuring
  charges, M&A transaction costs, natural-disaster losses, COVID-era anomalies.
  *Defensibility test:* has this type of item actually occurred only once in the
  look-back period, or does "one-time" recur every year under a different label?
  A charge that shows up in three of the last four years is not one-time.
- **Non-recurring revenue or pull-forward effects.** A large one-time customer
  order, a government stimulus benefit, channel stuffing near a period-end that
  inflates current-period revenue at the expense of the next period.
  *Defensibility test:* would this revenue have occurred in the ordinary course
  of business regardless of any incentive to hit a number?
- **Discretionary or deferred spending.** Underinvestment in marketing,
  maintenance capex, or headcount right before a sale process can inflate
  trailing EBITDA at the cost of understating the run-rate cost structure a
  buyer will actually inherit. This cuts EBITDA *down* from the reported figure,
  not up — watch for it as much as inflationary addbacks.
- **Accounting policy changes.** A change in revenue recognition timing,
  inventory valuation method, or capitalization policy mid-period can shift
  reported earnings without any change in the underlying business.

## Red flags that should stop the process, not just adjust a number

- **Revenue recognition irregularities**: recognizing revenue before delivery
  or before a right of return has lapsed, bill-and-hold arrangements without
  clear substance, recurring large adjustments in the last week of a quarter.
- **Related-party transactions without arm's-length documentation** — especially
  ones that move cash or margin between the target and an owner-controlled
  entity that will not transfer with the sale.
- **Unexplained or growing gap between reported net income and operating cash
  flow.** Earnings that consistently outpace cash generation deserve scrutiny of
  receivables, inventory, or revenue-recognition timing.
- **Auditor changes, going-concern language, or a qualified opinion** in recent
  financial statements.
- **Customer concentration paired with informal or handshake contracts** — an
  EBITDA base that depends on relationships that may not survive a change of
  control (route this to the Commercial Diligence Agent for the customer-side
  view; flag it here for the earnings-quality implication).

## Working capital normalization

A purchase price is typically set against a "normal" level of working capital,
with a true-up at close. Establish the target's normal net working capital from
a trailing 12–24 month trend (excluding known seasonal peaks/troughs and any
one-time swings), not a single point-in-time balance sheet. A working-capital
peg set incorrectly transfers value in either direction at close without anyone
intending it to.

## Output this workstream owes the Underwriting Agent

A single adjusted EBITDA figure is not enough. Hand off: (1) the reported
figure, (2) each adjustment with a one-line rationale and dollar amount, (3) the
resulting adjusted figure, and (4) a short list of items considered but
*rejected* as adjustments, with why — so the Underwriting Agent (and eventually
the human reviewing the memo) can see the full reasoning, not just the output.
