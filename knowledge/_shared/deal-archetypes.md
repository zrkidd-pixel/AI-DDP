# Deal Archetypes

Referenced by: Thesis / IC Memo Agent, Market & Sector Intelligence Agent.

Distinct from `value-creation/value-lever-taxonomy.md`, which covers what
you do to a company *after* you own it. This file covers *why the
opportunity exists in the first place* — the seller's situation and
motivation, which belongs in the thesis narrative's rationale, not the
100-day plan.

## The archetypes

- **Distressed investment.** The target faces financial distress —
  overleveraged, in covenant negotiations, or otherwise pressured to sell
  on a timeline dictated by creditors rather than a normal process
  `[source: Jenkinson, Kim & Weisbach, 2021]`. This is a specialist
  strategy requiring skills distinct from a standard buyout: acquiring or
  renegotiating debt tranches, restructuring fixed commitments (leases,
  supplier contracts), handling legacy liabilities. If this archetype
  applies, activate `extensions/distressed-diligence/` — this file
  identifies *why* the deal is available; that extension covers the
  additional diligence it actually requires.
- **Transition in ownership.** A founder- or family-owned business facing
  succession or wealth-realization pressure — the business grew and
  flourished without ever becoming public, ownership is concentrated in a
  few individuals, and there's no natural path to liquidity or continuity
  `[source: Jenkinson, Kim & Weisbach, 2021]`. A sponsor's value proposition
  here is bringing management expertise and enabling the founder(s) to exit
  over a period of time, not overnight — relevant context for
  `management-assessment/reference-check-framework.md`'s fit assessment
  and `management-assessment/rollover-incentive-conventions.md`'s rollover
  structuring, since founder engagement post-close (or lack of it) is
  often central to this archetype's actual risk profile.
- **Corporate orphan.** A business unit being divested from a larger
  conglomerate that has refocused on its core business `[source: Jenkinson,
  Kim & Weisbach, 2021]`. These units often suffer from board-level
  under-attention and capital starvation inside the parent — not because
  the underlying business is weak, but because it stopped being a
  priority. If this archetype applies, diligence should distinguish
  genuine underperformance from parent-driven underinvestment (a
  financial-diligence question, see
  `financial-diligence/qoe-redflag-checklist.md`'s point on discretionary/
  deferred spending) and confirm what carve-out mechanics (shared
  services, IT separation, transition agreements) the deal actually
  requires — route to Legal & Structuring.
- **Privatization.** A state-owned enterprise moving into the private
  sector `[source: Jenkinson, Kim & Weisbach, 2021]`. SOEs frequently lack
  autonomy, strong management incentives, and market access to finance; a
  sponsor can add value by improving management and providing capital
  access an SOE structure didn't allow. Rare in most funds' actual mandate
  scope — check `_shared/fund-mandate.md` before treating this as a live
  archetype rather than a background category.

## How this connects to the thesis

`thesis-icmemo/ic-memo-template.md`'s sector investment thesis section asks
"why should Fund II invest in this sector" — naming the actual archetype
(if one clearly applies) makes that answer concrete instead of generic. Not
every deal fits one of these cleanly; a straightforward sale from a
financial sponsor to another sponsor, for instance, doesn't need an
archetype forced onto it. Use this file when it genuinely clarifies the
deal's rationale, not as a checklist every memo must tick.
