# Value Creation Lever Taxonomy

Referenced by: Value Creation / 100-Day Planning Agent, Thesis / IC Memo Agent

Grounded in the academic literature on buyout value creation, not just
industry convention — see Sources below. Citations are load-bearing here:
when a proposed lever maps to one of these categories, name it explicitly
rather than reinventing looser language.

## The lever categories

- **Financial engineering (leverage).** The classic strategy `[source:
  Jensen, 1986]`: increasing leverage significantly, so debt service
  reduces free cash flow available for wasteful spending and focuses
  management on generating profits. **Caveat, not just history:** because
  essentially every GP can raise comparable leverage today, this is a
  baseline expectation, not a competitive differentiator, on its own
  `[source: Jenkinson, Kim & Weisbach, 2021]`. This lever is *set* by the
  Underwriting Agent and Financing/Capital Structure Agent as part of deal
  structure, not proposed independently here — this agent's job is to
  state it as thesis context (why the leverage level itself is part of the
  value-creation story), not to re-decide it.
- **Refocus operations (portfolio focus).** Identify and divest noncore
  assets, concentrating investment in the highest-margin, highest-growth
  parts of the business `[source: Jenkinson, Kim & Weisbach, 2021]`. Buyout
  ownership gives a fresh, un-legacy-encumbered read on which assets are
  actually core — distinct from margin improvement, since this is a
  portfolio-composition decision (sell/close a unit), not a cost action
  within a unit.
- **Enhancing executive management.** Replacing the Chair, CEO, or other
  senior executives, often bringing in an operating partner to chair the
  board `[source: Jenkinson, Kim & Weisbach, 2021]`. This is the direct,
  named response when `management-assessment/reference-check-framework.md`
  surfaces a material fit gap or key-person dependency risk — if
  Management Assessment flagged a gap, this lever should say so explicitly
  rather than proposing operational fixes around a management problem
  instead of addressing it.
- **Operational efficiencies.** Improving IT/management-information
  systems, increased outsourcing, renegotiating supplier contracts, closing
  less-efficient facilities `[source: Jenkinson, Kim & Weisbach, 2021]`.
  "Operational engineering" has become as important a competency as
  financial engineering, and more differentiating since it's harder to
  replicate generically. Also covers: procurement savings and
  shared-services consolidation (especially relevant when multiple owned/
  licensed brands or bolt-ons share back-office functions). Be specific
  about *why* the margin gap exists today (sub-scale procurement, redundant
  back-office, an inefficient plant) rather than asserting a generic
  "margin expansion opportunity."
- **Scale economies.** Roll-ups of fragmented sectors via a series of
  add-on acquisitions, often at valuation multiples below market leaders,
  plus accelerated organic expansion — domestically or by taking a
  domestically-focused business international `[source: Jenkinson, Kim &
  Weisbach, 2021]`. This is the direct value-creation expression of a
  fragmented-market consolidation thesis identified in Market Intelligence
  and Competitive Positioning — if the thesis says the sector is
  fragmented, this lever needs a specific, named pipeline of plausible
  bolt-on candidates (see `_shared/data-source-directory.md` for sourcing
  one), not just the assertion that fragmentation exists.
- **Revenue growth (organic).** Pricing, new product lines, channel
  expansion — distinct from the scale-economies lever above in that it
  doesn't depend on acquiring anything. Distinguish levers the current
  management team can execute from ones requiring new capability or
  leadership (tie to the management-enhancement lever if the latter).
- **Working capital and capital efficiency.** Improving cash conversion
  cycle, renegotiating supplier/customer payment terms, reducing excess
  inventory. The source literature treats this as an extension of the
  financial-engineering lever (increased revolving-credit-facility use
  alongside reduced free cash flow) `[source: Jenkinson, Kim & Weisbach,
  2021]`, but it's kept as its own category here since it's executable
  independent of the capital structure decision and frees cash without
  requiring operational transformation.

## Connecting recurring/connected-revenue shifts to value creation

Where the underlying business shifts revenue mix toward recurring or
subscription-like models, that shift itself often supports a higher exit
multiple independent of any margin or growth improvement — because
recurring revenue is priced by the market as more durable than one-time
sales. If this lever applies, tie it directly to the specific competitive
positioning and customer-concentration findings, not assert it generically.

## What makes a lever real vs. a slide-ware assumption

A lever belongs in the thesis only if it has: (1) a specific mechanism —
not "improve margins" but "consolidate sourcing across the three owned
brands to capture volume discounts currently unavailable to any single
brand"; (2) a rough sizing with a stated basis; (3) an owner and rough
timeline once in the 100-day plan. A lever without these three things is a
hope, not a plan — this applies equally to every category above, including
the ones with strong academic grounding. A citation to the literature
establishes that the *category* is a real value-creation mechanism in
general; it does not establish that *this specific deal* actually has that
opportunity.

## What the empirical split actually looks like

Bain & Company's analysis of global buyout deals (entry years 2013–23,
DealEdge powered by CEPRES data) decomposes realized enterprise-value
growth into revenue growth, margin expansion, and multiple expansion
`[source: Bain & Company, Global Private Equity Report 2024]`. Two findings
are directly useful when assessing whether a proposed lever mix is
realistic:

- **For the average deal, margin expansion contributes almost nothing** —
  indexed to 100 at entry, revenue growth contributed 27.4 and margin
  expansion only 0.1, against 24.0 from multiple expansion, for enterprise
  value growth to 151.5 at exit (53% revenue+margin combined vs. 47%
  multiple expansion). The industry has historically leaned on rising
  multiples and revenue, not on the operational-efficiency and
  management-enhancement levers above, despite those levers being
  available.
- **Top-quartile deals look different**, not just bigger: revenue growth
  35%, margin expansion 15%, multiple expansion 50% of value created.
  Bottom-quartile deals are net negative. Margin expansion specifically —
  the operational-efficiencies lever above — is the differentiator between
  an average deal and a top-quartile one, not a nice-to-have addition to a
  revenue-and-multiple story.

**Use this as a sanity check on a proposed lever mix, not a target to hit
mechanically:** if a thesis's value-creation plan is entirely revenue growth
and an assumed exit-multiple re-rating with no stated margin-expansion
mechanism, that mix resembles the average deal, not a top-quartile one —
worth naming explicitly to Thesis / IC Memo, especially in a market where
multiple expansion can't be assumed as a tailwind (see
`thesis-icmemo/returns-sensitivity-conventions.md`).

## Sequencing and prioritization

Not every lever should be pursued simultaneously. Prioritize by a rough
impact-vs-difficulty view: levers executable with the existing team and
capital structure come first; levers requiring new capital, new
leadership (management enhancement), or unresolved diligence questions
(an unconfirmed bolt-on pipeline) belong later in the plan, not in the
first 100 days.
